# OmniVoice at 32 steps: FP8, NVFP4 and shared GPU memory

RTX 5090 / TensorRT 11.2.1.2 / 27 September 2026. Full audio-file latency, not streaming time-to-first-audio.

FP8 is the best speed/memory balance in this small test: fastest automatic voice and no additional transcription errors. NVFP4 saves a further 183 MiB in engine storage and is fastest for cloned voices, but slower than FP8 for automatic voices and has one extra cloned-voice word error. Both remain experimental listening-test options. Shared-memory FP16 preserves the tested WAVs exactly.

## What changed

The shared-memory adapter frees the unused original transformer layers, normalization and audio head after TensorRT loads, retains embeddings and the original voice encoder/codec, and uses one TensorRT scratch allocation across serial contexts. Two shape graphs are retained with LRU eviction. Embeddings feed TensorRT through their existing GPU addresses; embedding preparation and float32 logit conversion are captured with the engine. Rotary positions are built once per shape. Static masks copy once per iterative request; only changing token IDs copy each step. Cached reference prompts avoid re-encoding and an unnecessary cache-hit synchronization. All measured OmniVoice requests used exactly 32 TensorRT calls.

## Correctness and memory ownership

The shared-memory FP16 path produced identical logits in 16 checks across lengths 32, 64, 96, 160 and 1536, including changed tokens and cache eviction. Its ten repeat-0 WAV files matched the original TensorRT WAV files byte for byte (five texts, auto and clone). Stable graph buffers and their contexts remain alive together. Scratch memory is shared only across serialized execution; concurrent calls must use separate scratch buffers or explicit serialization. The maximum-shape engine scratch requirement remains 822 MiB; querying shape-specific scratch did not reduce this engine requirement. A cache miss captures a new graph and can take seconds; these costs are recorded under warmups, not hidden in steady-state measurements.

## Precision and calibration

FP8 uses per-tensor E4M3 activation and weight quantization. NVFP4 uses E2M1 weights/activations with 16-element blocks, FP8 block scales and global scales; activations are dynamically block-quantized. Both engine inspectors show 196 genuinely low-precision GEMMs, with native FP8 or SM120 FP4 tactics. Calibration used six full 32-step generations: three separate calibration texts, seeds 42/17/123, both automatic and cloned voices. This is a small calibration set. Attention, normalization, residual paths and the output head remain at their original floating precision; the exported baseline actually contains mixed FP16/FP32 operations despite the historical FP16 label. The codec and voice reference encoder are unchanged.

## Speed and quality method

Five fixed texts, three warmed repeats per engine/mode; 135 timed requests. One resident engine at a time. Text blocks shuffled with seed 71; each text warmed immediately before its three measurements so the two-shape cache does not bias results. Medians combine five text lengths and are workload-specific, with no confidence interval from this small run. End-to-end time includes IPC and WAV writing. Whisper base.en transcribes repeat 0: 45 clips. WER is recognizer word error, not naturalness or speaker similarity. One synthetic bf_isabella Kokoro reference is reused; the dialogue test overlaps its transcript. Cloning inputs remain active, but human-clone fidelity and broad seed/language robustness are untested.

## Transfers and remaining work

GPU-to-GPU memcpy traffic fell from 41.05 MiB to 1.17 MiB (about 97%). CPU-to-GPU traffic stayed at 3,205 bytes in four events; one 280,320-byte audio download remained. No cudaMalloc calls appeared in any warmed profile. These CUDA memcpy totals exclude copy-like compute kernels. The profiler records a warmed cloned utterance separately from timing. The allocation/copy table below compares the original and shared-memory adapter. PyTorch aten::empty counts are allocator requests, not physical cudaMalloc calls. Graph replay hides operations from the CPU operator view, so reduced CPU operator counts alone do not prove fewer GPU operations. CUDA memcpy events give the transfer evidence. Reference encoding runs once; final decoded audio still returns to CPU for WAV playback. The sampler, codec and audio postprocessing still allocate tensors. Further opportunities are a fixed-shape sampling graph with controlled RNG, codec acceleration, and a smaller maximum-token engine profile. These were not silently changed in this experiment.
